D3WED

Master debits, credits & the accounting equation

Mission · Foundation theory · ~90 min
✓
🎬 The scenario

A client looks at her Balance Sheet and DMs you: "Mary, why does my bank account show as a CREDIT? Did something go wrong? I'm panicking."

You need to confidently reply: "Totally normal! 'Credit' in accounting doesn't mean 'bad' — it just means the right side of the entry. For your bank account on the Balance Sheet, the balance is a Debit (left side). What you're seeing is probably the increase indicator. Want me to record a 90-second Loom walking through it?"

💡 Why this matters

Every other day in this challenge depends on you understanding debits and credits. This is THE foundation. Watch the videos twice if you need to. Then take a quiz. Then teach it back to yourself. Don't move on until you can explain DEAD-CLIC from memory.

📋 Step-by-step playbook
  1. Watch "The Accounting Equation" by Accounting Stuff (8 min). Take notes.
  2. Watch "Debits and Credits — Once and For All" (10 min). Pause and rewind freely.
  3. Watch "The 5 Account Types" (9 min).
  4. In your journal, draw the DEAD-CLIC table from memory. Then check it: Debit increases: Dividends, Expenses, Assets. Credit increases: Liabilities, Income, Capital (Equity).
  5. Take 3 quizzes on AccountingCoach. Aim for 80%+. Redo until you hit it.
  6. For each of these 5 transactions, write out the debit and credit on paper:
    a) "We received $500 cash from a customer for services"
    b) "We paid $200 cash for office supplies"
    c) "We took out a $5,000 loan from the bank"
    d) "We paid the owner a $1,000 dividend"
    e) "We received a $150 utility bill, will pay later"
  7. Check your answers (full key in your journal — I'll trust you to look them up).
  8. Write the explanation for the client above. 3 sentences, plain English. Save it to your "Client Scripts" doc.
💬 Reply to the "why is my bank a credit?" panic DMTotally normal — promise! 💗 In accounting, "Credit" doesn't mean "negative" — it's just a label for the right side of every entry. Your bank account is recorded as a DEBIT balance on the Balance Sheet (left side, which is normal for cash). The "credit" you might be seeing is probably the action that INCREASED it (like a deposit being credited from the bank's perspective). I can record a quick 90-second Loom walking through it if helpful — just say the word!

D4THU

Build your first Chart of Accounts (real, not fake)

Mission · COA setup for a café · ~75 min
✓
🎬 The scenario

New client onboarding call. The owner says: "We just opened a coffee shop 6 months ago. We've been doing books in Excel. We need real accounting now. We have: rent, payroll, coffee beans, milk, gift cards we sell, tips employees collect, sales from coffee and food, a small loan from my dad, and we just bought an espresso machine for $8,000. Help?!"

If you can build her a clean Chart of Accounts in 30 minutes, you've earned her trust for the next 2 years.

💡 Why this matters

The Chart of Accounts is the spine of every set of books. A messy COA = messy books forever. A clean COA = everything else gets easier. This is one of the most undervalued skills — the bookkeepers who can build a good COA charge premium rates.

📋 Step-by-step playbook
  1. In QBO Sample Company, click Accounting → Chart of Accounts. Look at what's already there. Note the columns: Name, Type, Detail Type, Balance.
  2. Click "New" to add a new account. Practice creating ONE income account: "Coffee Sales" (Type: Income · Detail Type: Sales of Product Income).
  3. Now build the Sunrise Café COA. Create these accounts:
    Income: Coffee & Beverage Sales · Food Sales · Merchandise Sales · Tips Collected (yes, separate it).
    Cost of Goods Sold: Coffee Beans & Tea · Milk & Dairy · Pastries & Food Cost · Packaging.
    Expenses: Rent · Wages & Salaries · Payroll Taxes · Utilities · Marketing · Software & Subscriptions · Bank Fees · Office Supplies.
    Assets: Espresso Machine (Type: Fixed Asset · Detail: Machinery & Equipment).
    Liabilities: Gift Cards Sold (Type: Other Current Liability) · Loan from Owner's Father (Type: Long Term Liability).
  4. Wait — pause on Gift Cards Sold. Why is it a LIABILITY not income? Because the customer paid for coffee they haven't received yet. You OWE them coffee. Only when they redeem it does revenue happen. Write this in your journal.
  5. Wait — pause on Tips Collected. In most US states, tips that pass through the business to employees are recorded as a "pass-through" — often as a separate income account paired with a tip-payout expense, OR as a liability. Check the client's state rules. Default in QBO: track as a liability. Note this too.
  6. Run a fresh COA report (Reports → Chart of Accounts). Export to PDF. Save it to your Portfolio folder as "Sunrise_Cafe_COA_Day4.pdf."
  7. Screenshot the COA list view. This goes in your portfolio as your first "case study" artifact.
  8. Write a 1-paragraph "design note" in your journal: "Why I chose this structure for a café…". Real bookkeepers can DEFEND their COA choices to a CPA.
💬 What to say to the client when you deliver the COAHi [Owner Name]! I built your Chart of Accounts based on what you shared. A few highlights: • Gift Cards are tracked as a LIABILITY (we owe coffee) — they only turn into revenue when redeemed. • Tips are tracked separately so we can pay out employees cleanly each pay period. • Espresso machine is a Fixed Asset — we'll depreciate it over its useful life (your CPA can advise on the schedule). • Your dad's loan is a Long Term Liability — separate from short-term liabilities so it shows correctly on the Balance Sheet. I attached the full COA as a PDF. Once you confirm it looks right, I'll start entering your transactions. Any questions, just shout!

  • Don't classify gift cards as income at the point of sale. It's a LIABILITY until redeemed.
  • Don't forget to differentiate COGS (direct product costs) from operating expenses.

D5FRI

The 3 financial statements — read them like a doctor reads X-rays

Mission · Statement fluency · ~90 min
✓
🎬 The scenario

Future client emails you in tears: "Mary, I made $40,000 in sales last month but I have $200 in my bank account. How is this possible?? Am I being robbed?"

You need to know exactly which statement explains the gap — and how to walk her through it kindly. This question comes up at LEAST once with every client. Be ready.

💡 Why this matters

The 3 statements are the language of business. If you can read them, you can spot problems before they become emergencies. This is what separates a "data entry bookkeeper" ($5/hr) from an "advisory bookkeeper" ($50/hr).

📋 Step-by-step playbook
  1. In QBO Sample Co. → Reports → run Profit & Loss for the last 3 months. Read every line out loud. Yes, out loud.
  2. Run the Balance Sheet as of today. Confirm with your eyes: Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity. If it's not balanced, the books are broken.
  3. Run the Statement of Cash Flows. This is the magic one for the scenario above. Note the 3 sections: Operating, Investing, Financing.
  4. Watch "Cash Flow Statement — Simple" by Edspira (10 min).
  5. Write out 5 reasons a profitable business can have $200 in the bank:
    a) Customers haven't paid yet (AR is high) ·
    b) Owner pulled money out as draws ·
    c) Big inventory purchase (cash → inventory) ·
    d) Paid down a loan (operating cash → financing cash) ·
    e) Bought equipment ($8k espresso machine, anyone?).
  6. Run an AR Aging report in QBO. See how much is OWED to the business. That's a clue to the "broke but profitable" problem.
  7. Run an AP Aging report. See what's OWED OUT. Compare.
  8. Draft your 3-sentence empathy reply to the panicked client. Save it.
💬 Reply to the "I'm profitable but broke!" panicDeep breath, friend — this is one of the most common things small business owners hit, and it's NOT a sign anything is wrong. 💗 Quick explanation: Profit and Cash are NOT the same thing. Your P&L shows you made $40k in REVENUE last month — that's sales you've INVOICED. But if customers haven't paid yet (sitting in your AR), or you paid out a big chunk for inventory / a loan / equipment, the CASH leaves before profit "settles." I'm going to send you a 1-page Cash Flow Summary tomorrow that breaks down exactly where every dollar went. Spoiler: the money is somewhere — usually customer invoices or inventory. We'll find it together. Talk tomorrow! — Mary
🛠 Tools todayQBO Sample Co. · Edspira · journal
🚫 Don't do this
  • Don't conflate revenue with profit, or profit with cash. Three different things.
  • Don't say "your books are wrong" — they're probably fine, the OWNER is just learning what cash flow means.
  • Don't give tax advice ("you should write off…"). That's the CPA's job.

Invoicing & Accounts Receivable — the chase

Mission · Bill the client + chase overdue · ~75 min
✓
🎬 The scenario

Real client message Monday morning: "Hi Mary! We have $14,000 of unpaid invoices going back 4 months. Some customers are great, others ghosting us. Can you chase them this week and tell me what's collectible?"

This is one of the highest-impact things you can do for a client — and one of the easiest to get paid well for. Bookkeepers who handle AR well are kept for YEARS.

💡 Why this matters

$14,000 sitting in AR is $14,000 the business can't use. Cash flow is oxygen. If you up $5k of it through good chasing, you've "earned" your fee in a single week.

📋 Step-by-step playbook
  1. In Sunrise Café, create 8 invoices spread across the last 90 days (vary amounts $200–$1,500, vary due dates).
  2. Mark 3 as paid (Receive Payment → deposit to "Undeposited Funds" — we'll clear this Day 11).
  3. Leave 5 unpaid. Make 2 of them 60+ days overdue. One should be 90+ days.
  4. Run AR Aging Summary (Reports → Who owes you → A/R Aging Summary). Screenshot it.
  5. Run AR Aging Detail — this shows each customer + each open invoice. Export to PDF.
  6. Categorize the 5 unpaid invoices into:
    a) Friendly nudge zone (1–30 days overdue) ·
    b) Firm follow-up zone (31–60 days) ·
    c) Concern zone (61–90 days) ·
    d) Send-to-collections zone (91+ days).
  7. Draft 3 follow-up email scripts (templates below).
  8. In QBO, set up automatic invoice reminders: Settings → Account & Settings → Sales → Reminders → Set 3 reminders (Day 3 before due, Day of due, Day 7 after).
  9. Save all your AR scripts to your Templates folder. You'll reuse them forever.

The client is panicking. Their coffee supply will be cut off. You have 30 minutes to find the bill, pay it, document the fix, and reassure both the client and the vendor. This happens. Be ready.

💡 Why this matters

Missed vendor payments destroy supplier relationships, which destroys businesses. Bookkeepers who handle AP cleanly are insurance against this. Charge accordingly.

📋 Step-by-step playbook
  1. In Sunrise Café → Expenses → Bills → enter 5 bills (Bay Area Coffee Roastery $800 · Local Dairy $200 · Sunshine Power $450 · Rent $2,500 · POS Software $89).

  3. Run AP Aging Summary + AP Aging Detail. Screenshot both for portfolio.
  4. For the 70-day overdue bill: in QBO → Pay Bills → select it → mark paid (use checking account, today's date).
  5. Add a memo: "Paid following vendor escalation email [date]. Reviewed cash flow Tuesday." This is the kind of note a CPA will thank you for at tax time.
  6. Set up a recurring bill for Rent. (Save the bill → click "Make Recurring" → monthly.)
  7. Draft a "we missed it" email script to the vendor (template below).
  8. Draft a "here's what happened + how we'll prevent it" email to the client (template below).
